\begin{abstract}
We deploy a spiking neural network (SNN)-equivalent intrusion detection
system (IDS) on the STM32N6570-DK Neural-ART NPU.
By exploiting the mathematical equivalence between single-timestep ($T{=}1$)
SNN inference and INT8 quantized ANN inference, we compile a lightweight
MLP (trained on NSL-KDD, 5-class) to a general-purpose microcontroller
NPU without any neuromorphic hardware.
The resulting INT8 model runs in \textbf{0.4561\,ms} (2,192~inferences/sec)
at 800\,MHz, occupying 137.7\,KB Flash and 1.25\,KB RAM.
A comparison experiment with QCFS (Quantization-Clip-Floor-Shift) activation
reveals that the \texttt{Floor} operator is not implemented in the Neural-ART
NPU and falls back to CPU, adding 17.6\% latency overhead.
To our knowledge, this is the first publicly documented IDS deployment
on a general-purpose MCU NPU and the first hardware verification of
the $T{=}1$ SNN--ANN equivalence on commercial NPU silicon.
Code and data: \url{https://github.com/thc1006/SpikeIDS-MCU}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deep learning has transformed network intrusion detection, but deploying
these models at the network edge remains difficult. Edge IDS must operate
under tight power and latency budgets, and the dominant approach today
still relies on cloud offloading or high-end GPUs,
neither of which fits IoT or telecom CPE (customer premises equipment)
scenarios where per-device cost must stay below \$10.

Spiking neural networks offer a different compute paradigm. Because SNNs
process information as discrete spikes rather than dense floating-point
activations, they can be extremely energy-efficient when run on neuromorphic
hardware~\cite{roy2019towards}. The catch is that neuromorphic chips are not
commodity parts. Intel Loihi~\cite{davies2018loihi} is a research platform.
BrainChip Akida is available commercially but carries a price premium.
Neither is a standard MCU that an OEM can drop into a low-cost IoT gateway.

A recent line of theoretical work changes this picture.
Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025one} showed that when an SNN runs for exactly one
timestep ($T{=}1$) with zero initial membrane potential, the threshold-and-fire
operation reduces to a ReLU-like clamp, and the forward pass becomes
functionally equivalent to an INT8 quantized ANN (under matched quantization
grids; the equivalence is approximate in practice due to rounding
differences). The practical implication: any NPU that can execute INT8
matrix multiply and ReLU can run an SNN-equivalent model without
neuromorphic hardware.

We put this theory to the test. Using the STM32N6570-DK development board
(ARM Cortex-M55 at 800\,MHz, Neural-ART NPU rated at 600\,GOPS INT8),
we train an MLP for 5-class intrusion detection on NSL-KDD, quantize it
to INT8, and deploy it on the NPU through ST Edge AI Developer Cloud.
The model infers in 0.4561\,ms with a memory footprint under 140\,KB.

We also run a side experiment. The QCFS activation
function~\cite{bu2022optimal}, widely used in ANN-SNN conversion research,
should in principle be a better match for SNN semantics than ReLU.
We train and export QCFS models and compile them for the same NPU target.
The result is a practical finding that, to our knowledge, has not been
documented before: the Neural-ART NPU has no hardware support for the
\texttt{Floor} operator, so each QCFS layer triggers a
dequantize$\to$floor(float)$\to$requantize round-trip on the CPU,
costing 17.6\% more latency than ReLU despite QCFS achieving 3.3
percentage points higher accuracy.

Our contributions, in summary:
\begin{itemize}
  \item To our knowledge, we provide the first publicly documented
    hardware verification of the $T{=}1$ SNN--ANN
    equivalence~\cite{chen2025one} on commercial MCU NPU silicon.
  \item To our knowledge, we present the first publicly documented
    IDS deployment on a general-purpose MCU NPU (prior work used
    FPGAs~\cite{ieee2025event} or neuromorphic
    chips~\cite{zahm2024csiac}).
  \item We document that the \texttt{Floor} operator required by
    QCFS~\cite{bu2022optimal} is not supported in hardware by the
    Neural-ART NPU, identifying a concrete barrier for deploying QCFS-based
    SNN models on commodity NPUs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{SNN-Based Intrusion Detection}

Research on applying SNNs to network intrusion detection has grown rapidly
since 2024, though nearly all of it remains in simulation.
Wang et al.~\cite{nature2024csnn} proposed a convolutional SNN for IDS,
reporting accuracy above 99\% on NSL-KDD in software simulation, but did
not address deployment on constrained hardware.
Prajwalasimha et al.~\cite{ieee2025event} described an event-driven SNN-IDS
architecture targeted at edge IoT devices and tested it on FPGA logic; their
results show that spike-based encoding of network traffic features can
reduce dynamic power, but they reported no MCU or NPU deployment.
Mustafa et al.~\cite{pmc2025hybrid} combined recurrent and spiking layers
for anomaly detection in IoT networks, demonstrating that the temporal
dynamics of SNNs can capture sequential patterns in traffic data.
Most recently, Karthik et al.~\cite{nature2026transformer} proposed a
transformer-spiking hybrid that fuses protocol-aware attention with
spike-coded features. Their model achieves strong accuracy on CIC-IDS
benchmarks, but runs only on GPU and makes no attempt at edge deployment.

The only prior work we are aware of that deploys an SNN-based IDS on
physical hardware is the CSIAC report by Zahm, Nishibuchi
et al.~\cite{zahm2024csiac}, which used the BrainChip Akida AKD1000
neuromorphic processor.
They reported 98.4\% accuracy at approximately 1\,W power.
However, Akida is a purpose-built neuromorphic chip with native spike
processing; it is not a general-purpose MCU and costs significantly more
than the STM32N6 (the Akida development kit lists at \$499 versus
\$89 for the STM32N6570-DK). Our work targets the opposite end of the
cost spectrum: a commodity MCU NPU that OEMs already use for vision-based
products.

\subsection{ANN-SNN Conversion}

The theory of converting a trained ANN into an equivalent SNN has matured
over several stages. Bu et al.~\cite{bu2022optimal} introduced QCFS,
a quantization-aware activation function that maps ANN activations to
discrete spike counts. By training with
$\text{QCFS}(x) = \lfloor \text{clip}(x/s, 0, L) \rfloor \cdot s$
(where $s$ is a learnable step size and $L$ is the number of levels),
the resulting network can be directly converted to a multi-timestep SNN
with minimal accuracy loss. PASCAL~\cite{pascal2025} refined this approach
with spike accumulation and adaptive layerwise calibration, achieving
near-lossless conversion on ImageNet-scale models.

The most relevant result for our work is the $T{=}1$ equivalence shown
by Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025one}. They demonstrated that when the number
of simulation timesteps is reduced to one, the SNN threshold-and-fire
mechanism degenerates to a simple clamp, making the forward pass
approximately equivalent to an INT8 quantized ANN. This result
eliminates the need for
multi-timestep simulation and, critically, means that any hardware capable
of INT8 inference can execute SNN-equivalent computations.
NeuroFlex~\cite{neuroflex2025} has since shown that co-executing INT8 ANN
and SNN layers on the same accelerator can cut the energy-delay product by
57--67\%, suggesting that the boundary between ``ANN hardware'' and
``SNN hardware'' is blurring.

To our knowledge, this paper provides the first publicly documented
empirical validation of the $T{=}1$ equivalence on commercial NPU
silicon. We also test whether QCFS (designed for multi-timestep
conversion) offers any advantage when compiled for a $T{=}1$ NPU target,
finding that it does improve accuracy but at a hardware cost that the
current NPU architecture cannot absorb.

\subsection{Edge AI on MCU NPUs}

The STM32N6 is STMicroelectronics' first MCU with a dedicated neural
processing unit. Public deployments on this NPU span computer vision
(YOLOv8n, MobileNet, face recognition) and audio processing (YamNet,
audio event detection)~\cite{st_neural_art}. The NPU's hardware
operator set covers \texttt{Conv}, \texttt{Gemm}, \texttt{Relu},
\texttt{Add}, \texttt{Clip}, and \texttt{MaxPool} in INT8;
operators outside this set fall back to the Cortex-M55 CPU with explicit
cache-maintenance overhead for data transfers between the NPU and CPU
address spaces~\cite{st_neural_art}.
To our knowledge, no prior work has deployed an IDS on any
general-purpose MCU NPU. Our work fills this gap.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{$T{=}1$ SNN--ANN Equivalence}

A Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) neuron with membrane potential $V$,
weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$, and threshold $\theta$ operates as:
%
\begin{align}
  V[t] &= \beta \cdot V[t{-}1] + \mathbf{W} \cdot \mathbf{x}[t] \label{eq:lif1}\\
  S[t] &= \Theta(V[t] - \theta) \label{eq:lif2}
\end{align}
%
where $\Theta$ is the Heaviside step function and $\beta$ is the leak factor.
At $T{=}1$ with zero initial state ($V[0]=0$), the leak term
$\beta \cdot V[0]$ is zero regardless of $\beta$, so
Eq.~\ref{eq:lif1} simplifies to $V[1] = \mathbf{W} \cdot \mathbf{x}$.
The output spike then becomes:
%
\begin{equation}
  S[1] = \Theta(\mathbf{W} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \theta)
  \label{eq:t1}
\end{equation}
%
Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025one} showed that INT8 post-training quantization
(PTQ) produces a discretization that closely matches the threshold-and-fire
operation: the quantized ANN output $\hat{y} = \text{clamp}(\lfloor
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}/s \rceil, 0, 2^{b}-1) \cdot s$ approximates the
SNN spike output $S[1] = \Theta(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} - \theta)$ when the
quantization scale $s$ and threshold $\theta$ are matched.
In practice, this means any NPU executing INT8 \texttt{Gemm} + \texttt{Relu}
is performing SNN-equivalent computation, with accuracy gaps below 0.5\,pp
on standard benchmarks~\cite{chen2025one}.

\subsection{QCFS Activation}

The QCFS activation~\cite{bu2022optimal} bridges ANN training and SNN
spike encoding:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{QCFS}(x) = \left\lfloor \text{clip}\!\left(\frac{x}{s},\, 0,\, L\right) \right\rfloor \cdot s
  \label{eq:qcfs}
\end{equation}
%
where $s = \theta / L$ is the quantization step, $\theta$ is a learnable
threshold, and $L$ is the number of quantization levels.
Backpropagation through the non-differentiable $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$
uses a straight-through estimator (STE).
After BatchNorm fusion, the ONNX graph contains \texttt{Gemm},
\texttt{Clip}, \texttt{Floor}, and \texttt{Mul} operators.

\subsection{STM32N6 Neural-ART NPU}

The STM32N6570-DK pairs an ARM Cortex-M55 at 800\,MHz with ST's Neural-ART
NPU, rated at 600\,GOPS for INT8 workloads.
The NPU natively supports \texttt{Conv}, \texttt{Gemm}, \texttt{Relu},
\texttt{Add}, \texttt{Clip}, and \texttt{MaxPool} in INT8. Operators
outside this set are
dispatched to the CPU, incurring cache-maintenance overhead for
NPU$\leftrightarrow$CPU data transfers~\cite{st_neural_art}.

\subsection{Model Architecture}

We use a four-layer MLP with BatchNorm for 5-class classification
(\textit{normal}, \textit{DoS}, \textit{Probe}, \textit{R2L}, \textit{U2R})
on NSL-KDD~\cite{tavallaee2009nslkdd}:
%
\begin{align*}
  &\texttt{Lin}(41 {\to} 256) \to \texttt{BN} \to \sigma \to
   \texttt{Lin}(256 {\to} 256) \to \texttt{BN} \to \sigma \\
  &\to \texttt{Lin}(256 {\to} 128) \to \texttt{BN} \to \sigma \to
   \texttt{Lin}(128 {\to} 5)
\end{align*}
%
where $\sigma$ is ReLU (Path~B) or QCFS (Path~A).
The model has 111,365 parameters for ReLU and 111,368 for QCFS
(the difference is three learnable thresholds $\theta$, one per hidden layer).
At ONNX export, each BatchNorm is algebraically folded into its preceding
Linear layer, so the exported graph contains only \texttt{Gemm}+\texttt{Relu}
(Path~B) or \texttt{Gemm}+\texttt{Clip}+\texttt{Floor}+\texttt{Mul}
(Path~A).

\subsection{Training}

Both paths use identical training settings: Adam
($\text{lr}=10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$), cosine annealing over
80 epochs, batch size 512. We report results from a single seed (42)
throughout; we acknowledge this limits our ability to estimate variance
and plan multi-seed runs in future work. We apply inverse-frequency class
weighting because the training set is heavily skewed
(U2R has only 52 samples out of 125,973; Normal has 67,343). Without
class weighting, the model ignores U2R and R2L entirely.

For QCFS, we sweep the quantization level $L \in \{4, 8, 16\}$ and
report all three. The learnable threshold $\theta$ is initialized per-layer
from the 95th percentile of activation magnitudes observed on the first
training batch.

\subsection{INT8 Post-Training Quantization}

We quantize FP32 ONNX models to INT8 using ONNX Runtime static
quantization with MinMax calibration on 1,000 randomly sampled training
instances. Per-tensor quantization (\texttt{QInt8}) is applied to
weights and activations.

\subsection{NPU Compilation}

Models are compiled for the STM32N6570-DK using ST Edge AI Developer Cloud
v4.0.0 (compiler: Neural-ART \texttt{atonn} v1.1.3). The compiler
partitions the ONNX graph into NPU-executable epochs (hardware or hybrid)
and CPU-only epochs (software), generating C code that orchestrates
NPU$\leftrightarrow$CPU handoffs.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Dataset}

NSL-KDD~\cite{tavallaee2009nslkdd} contains 125,973 training and
22,544 test instances with 41 features. We use the standard 5-class
grouping: \textit{normal}, \textit{DoS}, \textit{Probe}, \textit{R2L},
\textit{U2R}. Three categorical features (\texttt{protocol\_type},
\texttt{service}, \texttt{flag}) are label-encoded with a combined
train+test vocabulary (necessary because the test set contains
category values absent from training; this mapping is a deterministic
bijection and does not leak label or feature magnitude information).
The remaining 38 continuous features are z-score
normalized using training-set statistics only.

Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} reports test accuracy for both activation
types at FP32 and INT8 precision.
QCFS $L{=}4$ outperforms ReLU by +3.30\,pp overall and +4.99\,pp macro.
We hypothesize that the floor operation during training acts as a form of
activation regularization, forcing activations onto a discrete grid and
reducing co-adaptation between neurons. Consistent with this hypothesis,
accuracy generally drops as $L$ increases
(79.75 $\to$ 78.79 $\to$ 79.39), trending toward the continuous ReLU
baseline (76.45). The non-monotonicity between $L{=}8$ and $L{=}16$
may reflect interactions with the class-weighting scheme; a more
controlled ablation study is needed to confirm the regularization
mechanism.

INT8 quantization causes minimal degradation for ReLU ($-$0.07\,pp).
ReLU outputs are non-negative, so the effective dynamic range is
half that of a signed int8 tensor, leaving more precision per value
and reducing quantization error.
QCFS suffers a larger drop ($-$1.70\,pp), likely because the floor-based
discretization during training imposes a grid that differs from the
MinMax calibration grid used by ONNX Runtime's PTQ, creating
a double-quantization effect.

Table~\ref{tab:npu} and Figure~\ref{fig:npu_breakdown} present
measurements from the STM32N6570-DK, obtained through ST Edge AI
Developer Cloud v4.0.0. The numbers tell a clear story.
ReLU INT8 maps cleanly onto the NPU: five hardware epochs handle all
\texttt{Gemm}+\texttt{Relu} layers, one hybrid epoch handles the output
layer, and only two software epochs are needed for input/output
marshalling. Total inference time: 0.4561\,ms.

QCFS INT8 runs the same \texttt{Gemm} operations on the NPU (13 HW
epochs, reflecting the finer operator granularity from \texttt{Clip},
\texttt{Floor}, \texttt{Mul} decomposition), but each \texttt{Floor}
node triggers a CPU fallback.
The compiler inserts \texttt{DequantizeLinear} $\to$ \texttt{Floor(float)}
$\to$ \texttt{QuantizeLinear} at every QCFS layer boundary, because the
NPU operates only on integer tensors and \texttt{Floor} is defined on
floats in the ONNX specification. This round-trip adds 0.08\,ms
per inference (+17.6\%), distributed across 14 software epochs.

QCFS FP32 runs entirely on the CPU (0 hardware epochs, 20 software
epochs) because the Neural-ART NPU only accepts INT8 operators. At
1.4156\,ms it is 3.1$\times$ slower than ReLU INT8.

\textbf{\texttt{Floor} operator: a documentation gap.}
ST's published operator support lists~\cite{st_neural_art} do not
explicitly mention \texttt{Floor}. Our experiment confirms empirically
that it is unsupported and quantifies the resulting performance penalty.
This finding is relevant to anyone attempting to deploy QCFS-based or
other floor-dependent SNN activations on the Neural-ART architecture.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Validating the $T{=}1$ Equivalence in Practice}

Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025one} established the $T{=}1$ SNN--ANN
equivalence theoretically and validated it on standard image
benchmarks (CIFAR-10, ImageNet).
Our result extends this to a non-vision domain (network security) and,
more importantly, to physical NPU hardware. The fact that the INT8
quantized ANN runs on the Neural-ART NPU at sub-millisecond latency
with only 0.07\,pp accuracy loss confirms that the equivalence is not
merely a mathematical curiosity but a deployable engineering technique.

That said, the equivalence is approximate, not exact. INT8 rounding
artifacts and the specific quantization calibration scheme (MinMax in
our case) introduce noise that differs from the idealized SNN threshold.
On NSL-KDD, this noise is negligible. Whether it remains so on
higher-dimensional inputs (e.g., raw packet payloads) is an open question.

\subsection{QCFS: Better Accuracy, Worse Hardware Fit}

The QCFS result presents an uncomfortable trade-off. QCFS $L{=}4$ beats
ReLU by 3.30\,pp overall accuracy, which is a meaningful gain for a
security application. But the 17.6\% latency penalty from \texttt{Floor}
CPU fallback makes it a poor choice for this particular NPU.

This is not a fundamental limitation of QCFS; it is a limitation of the
Neural-ART operator set. If ST adds \texttt{Floor} support in a future
NPU revision (or if a compiler optimization replaces \texttt{Floor} with
an equivalent integer shift, as NeuroFlex~\cite{neuroflex2025} does in
custom accelerators), QCFS could become the preferred activation for
SNN-equivalent deployment. Until then, ReLU INT8 is the pragmatic choice.

\subsection{Comparison with Akida}

Direct comparison with the Akida-based IDS~\cite{zahm2024csiac} requires
caution. The CSIAC report does not fully specify the classification task
or dataset split, making accuracy numbers non-comparable. Zahm et al.\
also used a dedicated neuromorphic processor with native spike support
and a different model architecture. Our 76.38\% on 5-class NSL-KDD
therefore cannot be directly compared to their reported 98.4\%. What \emph{is}
comparable is the deployment target and cost: we show that a \$89
general-purpose MCU (which OEMs already source for vision products)
can run SNN-equivalent IDS inference at 2,192 inferences/sec.
In a flow-based IDS pipeline where features are aggregated over
connection records (as in NSL-KDD), this translates to classifying
over two thousand flows per second, well within the traffic rates
of typical IoT gateway deployments.

\subsection{Limitations}

Four limitations bound our results.
First, NSL-KDD is a legacy benchmark derived from 1998 DARPA traffic.
It does not contain modern attack types (e.g., DDoS amplification,
lateral movement, encrypted C2 channels). We chose it for
reproducibility and comparability, not because it represents production
traffic. Any deployment based on this work should retrain on
contemporary data such as CICIDS2017 or CSE-CIC-IDS2018.

Second, we measure inference latency but not power. The STM32N6570-DK
does not expose per-subsystem power rails in a way that allows clean
NPU-vs-CPU power attribution. Power measurement requires a custom setup
with shunt resistors on the VCORE rail, which we leave to future work.

Third, we report accuracy and macro accuracy but not F1-score or
precision/recall, which are more informative for IDS evaluation
under class imbalance. Future work should report the full confusion
matrix.

Fourth, the model is a shallow MLP. Deeper architectures (e.g., 1-D CNN
or small transformers) might achieve better accuracy on the minority
classes, but would require verifying that all operators still map
to the NPU.

\subsection{Future Directions}

Several directions follow from this work.

\textbf{Modern datasets.}
Retraining on CICIDS2017 or CSE-CIC-IDS2018 would test whether the
hardware constraints we identified (model size, operator support) hold
for more complex traffic distributions.

\textbf{RTOS integration.}
The 0.4561\,ms inference time fits within a typical 1\,ms RTOS tick
(e.g., FreeRTOS at \texttt{configTICK\_RATE\_HZ}=1000). A natural
next step is to integrate the IDS model into an RTOS task on the
STM32N6 (e.g., under FreeRTOS or Eclipse ThreadX), feeding it features
extracted from a packet processing pipeline. The Cortex-M55 can handle
feature extraction while the NPU runs inference in parallel.

\textbf{Power measurement.}
A proper energy analysis, measuring Joules per inference on both the
NPU and CPU paths, would quantify the energy advantage of the $T{=}1$
approach and allow direct comparison with Akida's published power
figures.

\textbf{Other MCU NPUs.}
The NXP i.MX RT1180 (eIQ Neutron NPU), Renesas RA8 (Ethos-U55),
and Alif Ensemble (Ethos-U55) all have INT8 NPUs. Testing the same
model across these platforms would establish whether the $T{=}1$
deployment strategy generalizes beyond STM32N6.

\textbf{Multi-timestep exploration.}
Although $T{=}1$ is sufficient for equivalence, running $T{>}1$ timesteps
could be useful if the IDS processes temporal sequences of packets.
This would require accumulating membrane potential across timesteps,
which the current NPU cannot do in hardware but could be managed in
software with reasonable overhead if the timestep count is small
(e.g., $T{=}2$ or $T{=}4$).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We trained an MLP for 5-class intrusion detection on NSL-KDD, quantized
it to INT8, and deployed it on the STM32N6570-DK Neural-ART NPU.
Inference takes 0.4561\,ms. The model fits in 137.7\,KB of Flash.
These numbers confirm that the $T{=}1$ SNN--ANN equivalence is not
just theory; it works on real, shipping hardware.

The QCFS experiment adds nuance. QCFS trains better (+3.30\,pp), but
the \texttt{Floor} operator has no NPU support and forces costly CPU
round-trips. For now, ReLU INT8 is the right activation for this class
of hardware.

We believe this work opens a practical path for deploying SNN-equivalent
models on the large installed base of general-purpose MCU NPUs,
bringing neuromorphic-style efficiency to devices that cost under \$10.

All code, trained models, and NPU benchmark logs are publicly available
at \url{https://github.com/thc1006/SpikeIDS-MCU}
(DOI:~\href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18906060}{10.5281/zenodo.18906060}).
